Three ways to run a circuit

Ideal simulator

Computes the circuit's quantum state exactly, up to rounding. The only randomness is shot noise. Bell circuit: only 00 and 11.

qsim in the Canvas labs; AerSimulator() in Qiskit. Storing the full state doubles the memory with each qubit: about 30 qubits on a laptop.

Noisy simulator

The same, plus a noise model: errors after gates, wrong readouts, relaxation. Gives a few 01 and 10. It is a model of the device, not the device.

qsim 1.3.0 NoiseModel; AerSimulator.from_backend(...) builds one from a device's calibration data.

Real quantum computer

Physical qubits in a refrigerator. Noise is real and drifts. Jobs wait in a queue, and time is limited.

IBM's free Open Plan: up to 10 minutes of quantum time per 28 days.

Shot noise and the 3-sigma rule

A result with probability p, measured with N shots, gives a fraction that varies from run to run with standard deviation

σ = √( p (1 − p) / N )

3-sigma rule: a measured fraction more than 3σ from the prediction is treated as a real difference: under the model, a gap that large happens by chance only about 3 times in 1,000. It is evidence against the prediction, not proof of a particular cause.

Four times the shots halves σ. Shot noise shrinks with more shots; noise in the hardware does not.

Pause and predict

An ideal Bell circuit run with 400 shots gives 00 in 56% of them. The prediction is 50%. Is something wrong with the simulator?

σ = √(0.5 × 0.5 / 400) = 0.025, so 3σ = 0.075. The gap is 0.06, inside 3σ. Nothing is wrong: this is shot noise. With 10,000 shots the same 0.06 gap would be far outside 3σ (0.015) and would show a real problem.

What an IBM qubit is

  • A superconducting circuit (a transmon): a capacitor and a Josephson junction, a nonlinear inductor made of two superconducting layers with a thin insulator between them.
  • Its two lowest energy levels are |0⟩ and |1⟩.
  • It works only at about 0.01 kelvin, inside a dilution refrigerator.
  • A gate is a calibrated microwave pulse, tens of nanoseconds long. A measurement sends another pulse and reads the reflected signal.

Every one of these steps is slightly imperfect, which is where the noise comes from.

What the hardware really runs

IBM's Heron processors have a few native gates: rz, sx (√X), x and cz, and each qubit is wired to only two or three neighbours. The transpiler rewrites a circuit in native gates on connected qubits. The Bell circuit for ibm_pittsburgh:

Same gate up to a global phase (Operator.equiv, Module 3). The rz gates are done in software and add no error; the three √X pulses and the CZ do. Longer circuits, and SWAPs between distant qubits, mean more chances to go wrong.

T1 and T2: qubits forget

T1: a qubit in |1⟩ stays there with probability e−t/T1. T2: the phase of a superposition fades as e−t/T2, so the Bloch vector shrinks toward the axis.

A CZ takes 88 ns and T1 is about 67 µs, so about 760 CZ gates fit into one T1. Relaxation matters most for long circuits.

T1 also explains why readout errors are uneven: a qubit in |1⟩ can decay to |0⟩ during the 2.6 µs measurement, so P(read 0 | was 1) is larger than P(read 1 | was 0).

Readout errors: the readout matrix

For one qubit, put the true state in the columns and the recorded result in the rows:

A = [[1 − e0, e1], [e0, 1 − e1]],   e0 = P(read 1 | was 0),   e1 = P(read 0 | was 1)

Recorded = A × true

A qubit that is really in |1⟩ (true [0, 1]) is recorded as [e1, 1 − e1].

Two qubits

A = np.kron(A1, A0), qubit 1 first, the same order as a two-qubit state in Module 4.

ibm_pittsburgh

qubit 0: e0 = 0.34%, e1 = 1.46%
qubit 1: e0 = 0.20%, e1 = 0.83%
Readout alone turns 1.4% of Bell results into 01 or 10.

Gate errors: the depolarizing model

The simplest gate error: with probability λ the gate scrambles the qubits into a random state; otherwise it works.

ρ → (1 − λ) ρ + λ I / 4

A scrambled pair gives each of 00, 01, 10, 11 a quarter of the time, so a depolarizing error λ on the CNOT gives λ/2 different results in the Bell circuit.

In qsim and Qiskit Aer: depolarizing_error(lam, 2) added to "cx" in a NoiseModel.

Pause and predict

In IBM's run, 507 of the 970 equal results were 00 and 463 were 11. Is that imbalance evidence that the device favours 0?

00 is 507/970 = 0.523 of the equal results. With N = 970 and p = 0.5, σ = √(0.25/970) = 0.016 and 3σ = 0.048. The gap, 0.023, is inside 3σ, so this run alone is not evidence of a bias. (Real devices often do favour 0 slightly, because of T1, but 1,000 shots cannot show it here.)

So these counts cannot tell whether the device was noisier on the day of the run than its April snapshot says. More shots, and calibration data from the same day and qubits, would be needed.

Readout error mitigation

Recorded probabilities are p = A × true, so the best estimate of the true ones solves

A x = p  →  x = np.linalg.solve(A, p)
  • x holds quasi-probabilities: they add up to 1 but can be slightly negative. Keep them.
  • Mitigation removes the bias readout adds, not shot noise; the spread grows a little, so it needs more shots.

Suppression and mitigation

Error suppression changes the circuit before it runs (for example dynamical decoupling pulses on idle qubits).

Error mitigation corrects the results afterwards (readout mitigation; IBM's TREX; zero-noise extrapolation).

Pause and predict

As an illustration, mitigating IBM's counts with the April snapshot's readout matrix for qubits 0 and 1 gives 1.7% different results instead of 3.0%. (IBM's lesson does not say which physical qubits the run used, or give that day's calibration.) Which conclusion does the evidence support?

Readout errors of the snapshot's size would account for part of the noise, about 1.3 points; without the run's own calibration this is an illustration, not a measurement. The remaining 1.7% is more than the snapshot predicts for the gates (0.5%), but a mitigated value at 1,000 shots has a spread of about 0.005, so 3σ is about 0.014 and the gap (0.012) is inside it. The honest answer: there is insufficient evidence to say how much of the rest is extra gate error. "All of it is readout" and "the model is wrong" both go beyond the data.

Summary

  • Shot noise: σ = √(p(1 − p)/N). Gaps larger than 3σ are real.
  • Hardware runs native gates (rz, √X, CZ) on connected qubits; every pulse adds a little error.
  • Readout: recorded = A × true, A = np.kron(A1, A0). Depolarizing λ on the CNOT gives λ/2 different results.
  • A noise model from calibration data is a first guess; real devices drift.
  • Mitigation solves A x = p: less bias, a little more spread. State only what the data supports.
