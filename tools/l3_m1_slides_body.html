Module 1 · Noise and open quantum systems2

Why state vectors are not enough

  • Noise: a qubit touches its surroundings. Afterwards the qubit alone has no state vector of its own; its state is part of a larger, entangled one.
  • Missing information: a source that sends |0⟩ or |1⟩ at random, and does not tell you which, is not in any superposition. You need a way to write "|0⟩ with probability ½, |1⟩ with probability ½".
  • Part of an entangled pair: one qubit of a Bell pair, on its own, has no state vector either.

All three are handled by one object: the density matrix ρ. Everything you can measure on the qubit follows from ρ.

The density matrix

For a pure state, ρ = |ψ⟩⟨ψ|: the column ψ times the row ψ†. For a mixture of states |ψₖ⟩ with probabilities pₖ:

ρ = Σₖ pₖ |ψₖ⟩⟨ψₖ|
  • ρ is Hermitian, its trace is 1, and its eigenvalues are never negative.
  • Diagonal: the probabilities of 0 and 1. Off-diagonal: the coherences, which hold the phases.
  • Probability of outcome k: ⟨k|ρ|k⟩. Expectation value of an observable A: tr(ρA).

|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2:

ρ = ½ [[1, 1], [1, 1]]

|0⟩ or |1⟩, each with probability ½:

ρ = ½ [[1, 0], [0, 1]] = I/2

Same probabilities in the Z basis; only the coherences differ.

Pure or mixed: the purity

purity = tr(ρ²)
  • 1 for every pure state; less than 1 for every mixed state.
  • Smallest for the maximally mixed state I/d: 1/2 for one qubit, 1/4 for two.
  • |+⟩ and I/2 give the same Z-basis statistics, 50% each. Measure in the X basis to tell them apart: |+⟩ always gives +, I/2 gives + half of the time. Purity 1 against 1/2.

In code: np.trace(rho @ rho).real, or DensityMatrix(rho).purity() in qsim and Qiskit.

Same ρ, different recipes

Two different mixtures can give the same density matrix, and then no measurement can tell them apart:

½|0⟩⟨0| + ½|1⟩⟨1|   =   ½|+⟩⟨+| + ½|−⟩⟨−|   =   I/2

The third recipe has no randomness in it at all: take the Bell state (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 and keep only one qubit. The partial trace over the other qubit gives

tr₁ |Φ⁺⟩⟨Φ⁺| = I/2

The pair is pure; each half is maximally mixed. This is how noise works: the qubit becomes entangled with its surroundings, and the qubit alone looks mixed.

The Bloch ball

Every one-qubit density matrix can be written with a Bloch vector r = (x, y, z):

ρ = (I + xX + yY + zZ)/2,   x = tr(ρX), …
  • |r| = 1: a pure state, on the surface (the Bloch sphere of Level 1).
  • |r| < 1: a mixed state, inside the ball. I/2 is the center.
  • purity = (1 + |r|²)/2.
  • A mixture of states is the same mixture of their Bloch vectors: a point on the line between them.

Noise channels and Kraus operators

A channel is anything that can happen to a qubit: a gate, noise, or a measurement whose result is thrown away. It is described by a few matrices Kₖ, the Kraus operators:

ρ → Σₖ Kₖ ρ Kₖ†,   with   Σₖ Kₖ†Kₖ = I

The condition keeps the trace equal to 1. A unitary gate U is a channel with one Kraus operator, K₀ = U.

Example, the bit flip with probability p: K₀ = √(1 − p) I, K₁ = √p X. The state is left alone with probability 1 − p and flipped with probability p.

In qsim and Qiskit Aer: amplitude_damping_error, phase_damping_error, thermal_relaxation_error; each has a list of Kraus operators.

Amplitude damping: losing energy

K₀ = [[1, 0], [0, √(1 − γ)]]   K₁ = [[0, √γ], [0, 0]]

K₁ takes |1⟩ to |0⟩: the qubit gives its energy to the surroundings. With probability γ an excited qubit has relaxed.

After a time t, γ = 1 − e−t/T1. T1 is the relaxation time.

On the Bloch vector:

z → 1 − (1 − z)(1 − γ)
x, y → x, y · √(1 − γ)

Every state moves toward |0⟩, the north pole. The coherences shrink too, at half the rate.

Module 1 · Noise and open quantum systems10

Phase damping: losing phase

K₀ = [[1, 0], [0, √(1 − λ)]]   K₁ = [[0, 0], [0, √λ]]

No energy changes hands: the probabilities of 0 and 1 stay the same. But the surroundings learn a little about which one the qubit is, and the coherences shrink by √(1 − λ).

On the Bloch vector:

z → z
x, y → x, y · √(1 − λ)

Every state moves straight toward the z axis. |+⟩ becomes a mixture of |0⟩ and |1⟩: the superposition is lost. This is dephasing.

T1 and T2 together

/*CURVES*/

A real idle qubit has both. After a time t:

z → 1 − (1 − z) e−t/T1
x, y → x, y · e−t/T2

T2 is the coherence time. Amplitude damping with γ = 1 − e−t/T1 then phase damping with λ = 1 − e−2t/T2 + t/T1 gives exactly this: the relax() you write in the lab.

Left: T1 = 100 µs, T2 = 80 µs, the values of lab Steps 5 and 6.

Module 1 · Noise and open quantum systems12

Why T2 can be at most 2 T1

Amplitude damping alone already shrinks the coherences: by √(1 − γ) = e−t/(2T1). So even with no extra dephasing,

e−t/T2 ≤ e−t/(2T1)   so   T2 ≤ 2 T1

In the formula for λ, 2t/T2 − t/T1 is never negative when T2 ≤ 2 T1. With T2 > 2 T1 the phase damping would need a negative probability, so no channel of this kind exists.

Typical values today: T2 a little below T1 to a little above it. T2 = 2 T1 means the qubit has no dephasing beyond what relaxation causes.

Pause and predict

A qubit with T1 = 100 µs and T2 = 80 µs waits for 500 µs, five times its T1. Which starting state comes through unchanged, and where does every other state end up?

|0⟩ comes through unchanged: it is the lowest-energy state, and relaxation only moves states toward it. Every other state ends up very close to |0⟩ too: after 500 µs, z = 1 − (1 − z)e−5 ≥ /*Z500*/, and the coherences are multiplied by e−500/80 ≈ /*C500*/. This is why qubits are reset by waiting, and why circuits have to finish well within T1 and T2. (Real qubits are not at exactly zero temperature, so a small part of them sits in |1⟩; this course leaves that out.)

Measuring T1

/*T1FIT*/

Flip the qubit to |1⟩, wait a time t, measure; repeat for many waits. P(1) = e−t/T1, so ln P(1) is a straight line with slope −1/T1.

On the simulator with T1 = 100 µs, 1,000 shots per wait: the fit gives /*T1FIT_VAL*/ µs. Shot noise moves each point; the long waits, with few counts, are the noisiest.

T2 is measured in a similar way, starting from |+⟩. The single exponential is a simplified model: IBM's calibration T2 comes from a Hahn-echo experiment, and a free (Ramsey) decay without the echo can be faster. Module 3 uses echoes to suppress errors.

Fidelity and tomography

How close is ρ to the pure state |ψ⟩ you wanted? The fidelity F = ⟨ψ|ρ|ψ⟩: 1 if ρ = |ψ⟩⟨ψ|, ½ for I/2 against any pure state.

This course uses this squared form. IBM's lessons define the fidelity as its square root, √⟨ψ|ρ|ψ⟩, which gives 0.707 for the same case.

Tomography finds ρ from measurements: x, y and z from many copies measured in the X, Y and Z bases, each as (N₀ − N₁)/N. For X apply H first; for Y, S† and then H.

/*TOMO*/

State made by ry(1.1) then rz(0.7). With 1,000 shots the estimate lands just outside the ball (|r| and F above 1), which no real state can do; careful tomography then picks the nearest physical state. Shot noise is random and shrinks with more shots; a readout error is systematic: 3% on each bit shrinks every component by 1 − 2 × 0.03 = 0.94, however many shots you take.

Summary

  • ρ = Σ pₖ|ψₖ⟩⟨ψₖ| describes pure states, mixtures and parts of entangled systems; purity tr(ρ²) tells them apart.
  • One qubit: ρ = (I + r·σ)/2; |r| = 1 on the surface, |r| < 1 inside the Bloch ball.
  • Channels: ρ → Σ KρK†. Amplitude damping (T1) pulls states to |0⟩; phase damping removes coherences; together they give the T1 and T2 decays, with T2 ≤ 2 T1.
  • Real qubits: T1 and T2 of a few hundred µs, different on every qubit. Tomography rebuilds ρ; shot noise and readout errors limit it.
