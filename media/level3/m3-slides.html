Error suppression and mitigation

Better answers from noisy qubits today: change the circuit so less error happens, and correct the average afterwards.

Three ways to fight noise

what it doesexamplecost
suppressionchanges the circuit so that less error happensdynamical decoupling, better compilationno extra circuits; the extra pulses add a little error
mitigationruns extra circuits and corrects the average result afterwardsreadout mitigation, zero-noise extrapolationmore circuits and shots; larger statistical error
correctionfinds and fixes errors in each shot, while the circuit runsthe repetition code, the surface codemany qubits per logical qubit

Mitigation gives better expectation values, not better individual shots. It suits today's circuits; long computations need correction.

One expectation value, all module long

  • H and a CNOT make the Bell state (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2; the qubits wait 10 µs, as qubits do while others are busy.
  • H on both qubits, then measure: ⟨XX⟩ is the average of (−1)number of 1s. Ideally ⟨XX⟩ = 1.
Module 3 · Error suppression and mitigation4

Four kinds of noise, one at a time

The lab's noise: T1 = 200 µs and T2 = 150 µs while waiting; a detuning of 0.05 rad per µs; readout errors of 2% (0 → 1) and 4% (1 → 0); 2% depolarizing error on the CNOT.

Together: ⟨XX⟩ = 0.409 instead of 1. The detuning costs the most: a relative phase of 1 rad gives about cos 1.

Module 3 · Error suppression and mitigation5

Coherent and incoherent errors

coherent

The same unitary every time: an over-rotation, a qubit frequency slightly off (detuning), a steady coupling to a neighbour.

Errors add up in amplitude: N steps of angle δ give angle Nδ. They can be reversed by the right extra gates.

incoherent

Random from shot to shot and moment to moment: relaxation (T1), fast dephasing (T2), depolarizing gate errors, readout errors.

They add up in probability, and no gate undoes them. Mitigation can still correct their effect on the average.

Module 3 · Error suppression and mitigation6

The spin echo: the simplest dynamical decoupling

  • Wait half the time: each qubit turns by φ/2 around Z. Apply X: the next φ/2 now turns the state back. A final X restores the frame.
  • On the Bell state, X on both qubits changes nothing (|00⟩ + |11⟩ → |11⟩ + |00⟩), so only the unwanted rotation is affected.
Module 3 · Error suppression and mitigation7

Decoupling removes the detuning

With the echo, ⟨XX⟩ rises from 0.409 to 0.753, and it no longer depends on the detuning.

Without any detuning, the echo changes nothing (0.756 against 0.753): fast random dephasing does not reverse, and the two X gates add a little error.

Real devices use longer sequences, such as XY4 (X, Y, X, Y), which also cancel errors around other axes.

Module 3 · Error suppression and mitigation8

Pause and predict

Each shot has a slightly different qubit frequency, but in any one shot it stays the same during the wait. Can the echo help? And if the frequency jumps at random several times during the wait?

A frequency that differs from shot to shot but is steady during the wait is refocused shot by shot: each shot's second half undoes its own first half, so the echo restores ⟨X⟩ fully. This "spread" is common on hardware (slow drifts, low-frequency noise). Noise that changes during the wait breaks the mirror symmetry, and the echo removes only its slow part. Try both in tab 1 of the explorer.

Module 3 · Error suppression and mitigation10

Readout mitigation at work

  • With the echo and readout mitigation, ⟨XX⟩ goes from 0.753 to 0.853.
  • The corrected "probabilities" can be slightly negative: they are estimates of the true distribution, and averages computed from them are still good estimates.
  • A full matrix has 4n entries and needs 2n calibration circuits: fine for 2 qubits, impossible for 100. Real methods assume the qubits' errors are independent, or, like IBM's TREX (twirled readout error extinction), randomize the measurement so that one number per observable is enough.
  • A calibration measured with shots, and taken earlier in the day, is itself noisy and can drift.
Module 3 · Error suppression and mitigation11

Zero-noise extrapolation: add noise on purpose

  • A CNOT is its own inverse, so CNOT·CNOT·CNOT = CNOT: the same circuit, with about three times the CNOT noise (scale 3). Five CNOTs give scale 5.
  • Measure at scales 1, 3, 5; fit a line, a parabola or an exponential; read the fit at scale 0.
  • ZNE removes only the noise it amplifies: here the CNOT error, not the waiting.
Module 3 · Error suppression and mitigation12

Extrapolating to zero

Measured (with echo and readout mitigation): 0.853, 0.819, 0.786 at scales 1, 3, 5.

Zero-noise estimate: 0.869 (line), 0.870 (parabola). The same circuit with no CNOT error gives 0.870.

Still not 1: the dephasing during the wait is untouched by all three tools.

Module 3 · Error suppression and mitigation13

The price: statistical error

300 repeats of the experiment, 4,000 shots per circuit. The averages land close to the exact values, but the scatter grows: about 1.5 times for ZNE, so about 2.3 times the shots per circuit, on three circuits instead of one.

Mitigation trades bias for variance. The stronger the noise it removes, the higher the price; for large circuits it grows quickly.

Module 3 · Error suppression and mitigation14

With a device's noise

FakePittsburgh (ibm_pittsburgh, 17 April 2026), qubits 97 and 107, a 20 µs delay, 40,000 shots per circuit, simulated with Qiskit Aer.

The model has no coherent idle errors, so the echo changes ⟨XX⟩ only within its statistical error (±0.005). Readout mitigation helps most; ZNE little, because the CZ error is only 0.2%.

What remains is the dephasing during the delay: exp(−t/T₂) for the two qubits is 0.903.

Did it help? Judge each step

  • Compare with a value you know: an ideal value from a simulator, a symmetry, or a circuit run forwards and backwards (which should return to the start).
  • Report every estimate with its statistical error. A change well within the error of the difference is not evidence of a change (the echo on FakePittsburgh); it may be real, but the data cannot show it.
  • Check the fit: points that do not follow a smooth curve, or a value outside [−1, 1], mean the extrapolation cannot be trusted.
  • Say what is left: here, dephasing during the wait. Mitigation cannot beat the decoherence it does not touch.

Summary

  • Suppression changes the circuit: dynamical decoupling cancels coherent and slow errors while qubits wait, at almost no cost.
  • Mitigation corrects averages: readout mitigation inverts a calibration; zero-noise extrapolation amplifies the noise and extrapolates to zero. Both cost shots.
  • Neither removes everything; only correction makes arbitrarily long computations possible.
  • In the lab: 0.409 → 0.753 → 0.853 → 0.869 for an ideal value of 1.
